The 4 IELTS Modules at a Glance

The IELTS exam tests your English ability across four core skills. Listening and Speaking are the same for all candidates, while Reading and Writing differ between Academic and General Training.

🎧

Listening

30 min + 10 min transfer

4 Parts · 40 Questions · Same for Academic & General Training. You hear each recording only once.

📖

Reading

60 minutes

3 Sections · 40 Questions · Texts differ for Academic (long academic passages) vs. General (everyday & workplace texts).

✍️

Writing

60 minutes

2 Tasks · Task 1 differs (graph vs. letter). Task 2 is an essay for both versions. Task 2 is worth twice the marks of Task 1.

🗣️

Speaking

11–14 minutes

3 Parts · Face-to-face interview with a certified examiner. Same for Academic & General Training. May be on a different day.

Module 1: Listening

30 min + 10 min · 40 Questions · 4 Parts

The Listening test is the same for both Academic and General Training. You will listen to four recordings of native English speakers and answer 10 questions per recording. The recordings are played only once, so concentration is critical. The difficulty increases progressively from Part 1 (easiest) to Part 4 (hardest). You get 10 extra minutes at the end to transfer your answers to the answer sheet (paper-based test only).

📋 The 4 Parts of Listening in Detail

Part Format Context Example Scenario Difficulty
Part 1 Conversation between 2 people Everyday social context Booking a hotel room, enquiring about a gym membership, renting an apartment, opening a bank account ⭐ Easiest
Part 2 Monologue (1 speaker) Everyday social context A tour guide describing a museum, a radio announcement about local events, a manager explaining workplace facilities ⭐⭐ Easy-Medium
Part 3 Conversation between up to 4 people Educational / training context A university tutor discussing an assignment with 2–3 students, a group planning a research project ⭐⭐⭐ Medium-Hard
Part 4 Monologue (1 speaker) Academic subject A university lecture on marine biology, a presentation on urban planning, a talk about historical architecture ⭐⭐⭐⭐ Hardest

❓ All Listening Question Types (6 Types)

These question types can appear in any of the 4 parts. You must be prepared for all of them.

1 Multiple Choice

Choose the correct answer from 3 options (A, B, C). Sometimes you may need to pick 2 answers from 5 options or 3 answers from 7 options. Tests your ability to understand detailed information and opinions.

💡 Tip: Watch out for distractors — the speaker may mention ALL options but only one is correct. Listen for words like "however," "actually," and "but."

2 Matching

Match a list of items (e.g., people, places, events) from the recording to a set of options on the question paper. Tests your ability to follow a conversation and identify relationships between facts.

💡 Tip: Read all options before the audio starts. The speakers often paraphrase, so listen for synonyms, not exact words.

3 Plan / Map / Diagram Labelling

Complete labels on a plan (e.g., a building), a map (e.g., a town), or a diagram (e.g., a piece of equipment). You choose answers from a list or write directly on the visual.

💡 Tip: Learn directional language: "to the left of," "opposite," "adjacent to," "in the top-right corner," "just past the bridge."

4 Form / Note / Table / Flow-chart / Summary Completion

Fill in the gaps in an outline of part or all of the listening text. The outline may be a form (e.g., application form), notes, a table, a flow-chart, or a summary. You write 1–3 words and/or a number.

💡 Tip: Pay close attention to the word limit! If it says "NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS," writing three words = wrong answer, even if the information is correct.

5 Sentence Completion

Fill in a gap in a sentence using information from the recording. You must write the exact words you hear. The word limit is specified (e.g., "NO MORE THAN THREE WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER").

💡 Tip: The answer is always in the same order as the questions. Use the words before and after the gap to predict the type of answer (noun, number, adjective).

6 Short Answer Questions

Read a question and write a short answer using information from the recording. Usually requires 1–3 words and/or a number. Questions often begin with "What," "Where," "When," "How many," or "Who."

💡 Tip: Spelling counts! If you misspell the word, you lose the mark. Practice spelling common IELTS vocabulary (days, months, addresses, academic terms).
📖

Module 2: Reading

60 min · 40 Questions · 3 Sections

The Reading test has 3 sections with a total of 40 questions. You have 60 minutes (no extra transfer time — you must write answers directly on the answer sheet). The texts and question difficulty increase from Section 1 to Section 3. The content differs between Academic and General Training.

📘 Academic Reading

  • 3 long texts (2,150–2,750 words total)
  • Topics: science, history, sociology, technology, environment, etc.
  • Texts taken from books, journals, magazines, and newspapers
  • At least one text contains logical argument
  • May include diagrams, graphs, or illustrations
  • Designed for candidates applying to university

📗 General Training Reading

  • Section 1: 2–3 short factual texts (e.g., timetables, advertisements, notices)
  • Section 2: 2 short work-related texts (e.g., job descriptions, staff policies, contracts)
  • Section 3: 1 longer, more complex text on a general interest topic
  • Texts taken from everyday English-speaking environments
  • Designed for candidates applying for immigration or work

❓ All Reading Question Types (11 Types)

The Reading module has the most question types of any IELTS module. You must master all 11.

1 Multiple Choice

Choose the best answer from 4 options (A–D). May also be a "choose 2 from 5" or "choose 3 from 7" format. Tests detailed understanding and ability to identify the writer's main point.

💡 Tip: Eliminate obviously wrong answers first. Look for paraphrasing — the correct answer will use different words than the text.

2 True / False / Not Given

Decide if statements agree with the factual information in the text. True = agrees, False = contradicts, Not Given = no information provided. This is one of the trickiest question types.

💡 Tip: "False" means the text says the opposite. "Not Given" means the text doesn't mention it at all. Don't use your own knowledge!

3 Yes / No / Not Given

Similar to True/False/Not Given, but tests the writer's views or claims (opinions) rather than facts. Yes = agrees with the writer, No = contradicts the writer, Not Given = the writer doesn't express a view.

💡 Tip: Focus on the writer's attitude and opinion words: "believes," "argues," "suggests," "claims." Don't confuse facts with opinions.

4 Matching Information

Find the specific paragraph (A, B, C, D…) that contains certain information (e.g., "a comparison," "a reason," "an example," "a prediction"). The instruction says: "Which paragraph contains the following information?"

💡 Tip: Do this question last for the passage. By then, you'll be familiar with the content of each paragraph. Note: some paragraphs may be used more than once.

5 Matching Headings

Match a list of headings (i–x) to the correct paragraphs or sections of the text. There are always more headings than paragraphs, so some headings are distractors. Tests your ability to identify the main idea.

💡 Tip: Read the first and last sentence of each paragraph to find the main idea. Don't match based on a single keyword — look for the overall meaning.

6 Matching Features

Match a set of statements or pieces of information to a list of options (e.g., match discoveries to scientists, match events to time periods, match characteristics to categories).

💡 Tip: Scan the text for the names/dates/categories in the option list first, then read around them to find the matching statement.

7 Matching Sentence Endings

You are given the first half of a sentence and must choose the correct ending from a list of options. Tests your understanding of the meaning and grammar of the sentence within the text.

💡 Tip: Check that the completed sentence is grammatically correct. This can help you eliminate wrong endings even before checking the text.

8 Sentence Completion

Complete sentences with words taken directly from the text. A word limit is given (e.g., "NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS from the passage"). Tests your ability to locate specific details.

💡 Tip: The answers appear in the same order as the questions in the text. Use keywords from the incomplete sentence to scan for the answer location.

9 Summary / Note / Table / Flow-chart Completion

Complete a summary of part of the text. You may need to choose words from a box of options or select words directly from the passage. The summary may use synonyms and paraphrasing.

💡 Tip: Identify the part of the passage the summary refers to first. Then match the meaning, not just keywords. Grammar clues (e.g., "a/an/the") help narrow choices.

10 Diagram Label Completion

Label a diagram based on information in the text. The diagram could be a machine, a natural process, a building, or a biological structure. Write the correct words from the passage.

💡 Tip: Look for the description of the process or object in the text. The labels usually follow the order of the description.

11 Short Answer Questions

Answer questions about factual details in the text using words taken directly from the passage. A word limit is specified (e.g., "NO MORE THAN THREE WORDS").

💡 Tip: Pay attention to question words: "What" = thing, "Where" = place, "When" = time, "How many" = number. This tells you what type of answer to look for.
✍️

Module 3: Writing

60 min · 2 Tasks · Min 150 + 250 words

The Writing test has 2 tasks. You must complete both. Task 2 is worth twice as many marks as Task 1, so spend about 20 minutes on Task 1 and 40 minutes on Task 2. You are assessed on 4 criteria: Task Achievement/Response, Coherence & Cohesion, Lexical Resource, and Grammatical Range & Accuracy (each 25%).

📝 Task 1 (Minimum 150 words · ~20 minutes · 1/3 of marks)

📘 Academic: Describe Visual Data 📗 General: Write a Letter
Academic Task 1 Types:
  • Line Graph – Describe trends over time (increase, decrease, fluctuation, plateau)
  • Bar Chart – Compare quantities across categories or time periods
  • Pie Chart – Describe proportions and percentages of a whole
  • Table – Summarize and compare numerical data in rows and columns
  • Process Diagram – Describe the stages of a natural or man-made process (e.g., how coffee is produced)
  • Map – Compare two maps of the same area at different times (e.g., a town in 1990 vs. 2020) and describe changes
  • Multiple Charts – Combine 2 different types (e.g., a pie chart + a table) and describe both with connections
General Training Task 1 Letter Types:
  • Formal Letter – To someone you don't know (e.g., a manager, a company). Use "Dear Sir/Madam" and "Yours faithfully."
  • Semi-formal Letter – To someone you know but not closely (e.g., a landlord, a professor). Use "Dear Mr. Smith" and "Yours sincerely."
  • Informal Letter – To a friend or family member. Use "Dear John" and "Best regards / Warm wishes."
  • Common purposes: complaining, requesting information, applying for a job, apologizing, inviting, thanking, explaining a situation

📝 Task 2 – Essay (Minimum 250 words · ~40 minutes · 2/3 of marks)

📘 Same for Academic & General ⚠️ Worth DOUBLE Task 1

You must write a formal essay in response to a point of view, argument, or problem. There are 5 main essay types:

1 Opinion Essay (Agree / Disagree)

"To what extent do you agree or disagree?"

You must clearly state your position and support it with reasons and examples throughout the essay. You can fully agree, fully disagree, or partially agree.

2 Discussion Essay (Discuss Both Views)

"Discuss both views and give your own opinion."

You must discuss both sides of the argument fairly in separate body paragraphs, then clearly state your own opinion (usually in the conclusion or a separate paragraph).

3 Problem & Solution Essay

"What are the causes of this problem and what solutions can you suggest?"

Identify 2–3 causes/reasons for the problem, then propose 2–3 practical solutions. Each body paragraph should focus on either a cause or a solution.

4 Advantages & Disadvantages Essay

"Do the advantages outweigh the disadvantages?" or "Discuss the advantages and disadvantages."

Discuss the positive and negative aspects of a trend or development. If asked "outweigh," you must clearly state which side is stronger.

5 Two-Part (Double) Question Essay

"Why is this happening? Is this a positive or negative development?"

Two separate questions are asked. You must answer both fully. Dedicate one body paragraph to each question. Failing to address both = lower Task Response score.

🗣️

Module 4: Speaking

11–14 min · 3 Parts · Face-to-Face Interview

The Speaking test is a face-to-face interview with a certified IELTS examiner. It is the same for Academic and General Training. The test is recorded for quality control. You are assessed on 4 criteria (each 25%): Fluency & Coherence, Lexical Resource, Grammatical Range & Accuracy, and Pronunciation. The test may be taken on the same day as the other modules or up to 7 days before/after.

Part 1: Introduction & Interview

⏱️ 4–5 minutes

The examiner introduces themselves and asks you to confirm your identity. Then they ask general questions about familiar topics. You should answer in 2–3 sentences per question — not too short, not too long.

Common Topics:

  • 🏠 Your home / hometown / accommodation
  • 💼 Work or studies
  • 🎵 Hobbies, music, sports, reading
  • 🍕 Food, cooking, restaurants
  • 🌤️ Weather, seasons
  • 🚗 Transport, daily routine
  • 👨‍👩‍👧 Family, friends
  • 🎨 Art, photography, colors
  • 🛍️ Shopping, fashion, gifts

Part 2: Long Turn (Cue Card)

⏱️ 3–4 minutes (1 min preparation + 1–2 min speaking)

The examiner gives you a task card (cue card) with a topic and 3–4 bullet points to cover. You have 1 minute to prepare (you can make notes on paper provided) and then must speak for 1–2 minutes without interruption. The examiner may ask 1–2 brief follow-up questions at the end.

Common Cue Card Topics:

  • Describe a person you admire / a famous person / a family member
  • Describe a place you visited / your dream holiday / a beautiful city
  • Describe an object you own / a gift you received / something you lost
  • Describe an event / a celebration / a memorable experience
  • Describe a book / movie / song / website you enjoy
  • Describe a skill you learned / a goal you achieved / a difficult decision
💡 Tip: Use the 1-minute preparation wisely. Write down keywords only (not full sentences). Structure your talk: Introduction → Point 1 → Point 2 → Point 3 → Conclusion/Feelings.

Part 3: Two-Way Discussion

⏱️ 4–5 minutes

The examiner asks deeper, more abstract questions related to the topic from Part 2. This is the most challenging part. You should give extended answers (4–6 sentences) with reasons, examples, and comparisons. This part tests your ability to express and justify opinions, analyze, speculate, and discuss issues.

Example (if Part 2 was about a holiday):

  • "Why do you think tourism is important for a country's economy?"
  • "How has travel changed compared to 50 years ago?"
  • "Do you think international travel will become more or less popular in the future?"
  • "What are the negative effects of mass tourism on local communities?"
💡 Tip: Use advanced structures: conditionals ("If governments invested more…"), comparisons ("Unlike in the past…"), hedging ("It could be argued that…"), and speculation ("I imagine that in the future…").
